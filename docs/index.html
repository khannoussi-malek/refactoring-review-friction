Methods paper · mining software repositories

The record does not trace the work.

Empirical software engineering reads a project's own commit messages and issue tracker and assumes they are a faithful trace of what happened. This paper measures that assumption in three record channels across two ecosystems, and it fails in every one.

Malek Khannoussi · Independent Researcher, Tunisia · preprint, IEEE format

Commit‑side traceability rate, 38 probed Apache projects

Each dot is one project. The bar — 0.80 of commits citing a tracker key — was written into predictions/PREDICTIONS.md before a single repository was cloned, and never moved. Twelve dots sit to its right. Hover any dot for its rate.

Two things follow, and the second is the one that is easy to miss. A selection criterion built on the published rate will admit projects that cannot support a ticket‑anchored study. And most of the gap between the two rates is arithmetic, not indiscipline — a tracker accumulates tickets faster than a repository accumulates commits, and below one commit per ticket the two rates are not comparable quantities at all.

§ III‑A
Method

Two traceability rates, and only one of them gets published

The literature reports one number. Issue‑anchored studies depend on a different one. They are not the same measurement pointed two ways — they have different denominators, and they diverge in the direction that flatters the corpus.

Fix a project p with a set of Jira keys, a repository observed at a pinned commit, and a tracker read at a snapshot time. Write cites(c) for the issue keys appearing in commit c's message. Nothing else is read: not the diff, not the branch name, not a pull request body, not the tracker's own link fields.

Definition 1 · commit‑side rate (CSR)

CSR(p) = |{c : cites(c) ∩ Kp ≠ ∅}|
            ―――――――――――――
            |{c : c → Hp}|

Of the commits in this repository, what fraction name a ticket? Answers: if I start from a commit, can I find its ticket?

This is what SEOSS 33 publishes as “Linked Change Sets [%]”, what Rath et al. report as “≈48% of commits were not linked”, and what the eligibility bar in this study is defined on. Not novel here.

Definition 2 · ticket realisation rate (TRR)

TRR(p) = |{k ∈ Tickets(p,T) : ∃c, k ∈ cites(c)}|
            ―――――――――――――――――――
                 |Tickets(p,T)|

Of the tickets in this tracker, what fraction are ever cited by a commit? Answers: if I start from a ticket, can I find the work?

Rath et al. measure this per issue type for six projects. The name is introduced here because the quantity has been reported without one, and its divergence from CSR has never been framed as a constraint on corpus selection.

“Realisation” is a claim about the record, not about the work

A ticket counts as realised when the repository's own record points back to it. A ticket can be resolved, implemented and shipped without any commit naming it, and this measure counts it as unrealised. That is the intended reading: it measures what a researcher can recover, not what a project did. Nothing here says a project on GitHub Issues is badly run — the Jira‑citation convention is a research affordance, and its decline is a cost to researchers rather than to maintainers.

The worked case: Apache Hive

Hive is the headline because it is not a discipline problem. By any published selection criterion it is close to perfect, and it still leaves nearly half its tracker invisible to a design that samples tickets.

The tempting reading of row 1 against row 3 is “Hive files tickets it never works on.” Row 2 says otherwise. Given 0.61 commits per ticket, Hive's ticket‑side rate could not have exceeded 59.6% at any level of discipline whatsoever, and it reaches 94% of that. The gap is a property of two artifacts accumulating at different speeds, not of the people using them.

Two instantiations, and the subscripts are load‑bearing

TRR compares a tracker read at time T against a repository read at Hp, and requires T ≤ date(Hp). If the tracker runs ahead of the repository, the newest tickets are structurally incapable of being realised and TRR is biased downward by an artefact that looks exactly like poor traceability.

TRRlive — Apache Jira read 2026‑07‑25, issue keys only, against shas pinned the same day. Covers the 12 eligible projects. Slight underestimate.
TRRfrozen — the Public Jira Dataset (Zenodo 15719919), a snapshot predating every pinned sha, aligned in issue‑number rather than date space. Covers all 38. Carries its own estimator error, quantified in § IV‑D.
An unsubscripted TRR, ceiling or fill in the paper means the quantity in general. Every measured value carries its subscript, and where a project appears with two numbers for what looks like one quantity, the subscript is the difference.

§ III‑A4
The bound

The arithmetic ceiling, and what is left once you remove it

CSR and TRR are not independent quantities. The constraint between them is exact, not statistical — and it is the paper's most portable contribution, because it costs nothing to compute and it bounds a study before any measurement is taken.

A commit either cites no key of p, contributing nothing, or cites at least one — and however many it cites, it can add at most one ticket that no earlier commit had already cited. So the number of distinct realised tickets is bounded by the number of citing commits, and dividing through by the tracker size:

TRR(p)  ≤  CSR(p)  ×  commits / tickets  ≡  ceiling(p)

tight — equality holds when citing commits map one‑to‑one onto distinct previously‑uncited tickets

Two consequences, and the second is why it matters.

First, commits‑per‑ticket is a scale factor the two rates do not share. A project with 0.16 commits per ticket cannot exceed a 16% ticket‑side rate at any commit‑side rate, including 100%. Comparing CSR to TRR without it compares a ratio to a ratio with a different denominator.

Second, it splits TRR into a part that is arithmetic and a part that is behaviour. Define fill = TRR / ceiling ∈ [0,1] — the share of the attainable maximum actually reached, i.e. how efficiently a project's citing commits spread across distinct tickets rather than piling onto a few. Fill is the quantity a claim about citation discipline needs. Ceiling is a property of how much code the project writes per ticket it files.

What the corpus actually looks like once you decompose it

Across the twelve eligible projects, under the live measurement:

— ceilinglive binds for every one of them, running 13.7% (Kylin) to 81.6% (Ranger) — a 6.0× spread;
— filllive is nearly flat: median 0.89, range 0.80 (Ranger) to 0.95 (Drill) — a 1.19× spread;
— so the 5.8× spread in TRR is 6.0× ceiling and 1.19× fill. Almost all of the variation is arithmetic.

Ranked by fill instead of by rate, the ordering changes almost completely — Drill (43.2%, fill 0.95) sits above Ranger (65.4%, fill 0.80). Every project in this corpus is reaching most of what its commit supply permits. Below one commit per ticket the two rates are not commensurable, and every eligible project is below it.

Where the ceiling stops being a ceiling

Under the frozen snapshot, three of the twelve have ceilingfrozen at or above 100% — Ozone 176.0%, Ranger 130.4%, Knox 100.0% — where a bound above one constrains nothing. That is not a disagreement about the projects. It is what a 2026 commit window measured against an older tracker produces: the repository holds more citing commits than the snapshot holds tickets. The two estimators bound different denominators. Neither figure is withdrawn.

Kylin is flagged, not featured — and that correction is itself a result

Earlier drafts led with Kylin: 83.9% commit‑side against 12.0% ticket‑side, “seven of every eight tickets with no commit at all.” That example is withdrawn. The pinned commit reaches 968 commits beginning 2022‑08‑01 — 7.5% of the 12,937 on the repository's refs — because the apache/kylin default branch was re‑initialised for Kylin 5 and the earlier history lives on other branches. So the 12.0% is a four‑year commit window measured against a twelve‑year, 5,931‑issue tracker.

Its filllive is 0.87, squarely at the corpus median. By the measure that isolates discipline, Kylin is unremarkable. Under the frozen estimator it reads 0.04% because 99.72% of its cited keys postdate the snapshot — the same defect from the other side. Live, the window is too short for the tracker; frozen, the tracker is too old for the window.

Kylin is retained in every table, because the bar was applied before any of this was known and removing it afterwards would be selection on the outcome. Three other projects have a pinned branch starting after their repository — Jena (49.0% of refs), Karaf (45.4%), James (89.4%, by eight days) — and none approaches Kylin's severity.

One note about the live correlations

All four of the paper's published coefficients reproduce from these artifacts: −0.010 (CSR against TRRfrozen, n = 33), −0.717 (CSR against commits-per-ticket, n = 33), +0.5175 against a published +0.518, and +0.4545 against a published +0.455 — the last two are rounding of the coefficient, not disagreement. An earlier version of this page reported the fourth as +0.473 and blamed a different commits-per-ticket basis. That tie does not exist in the data, and it was the entire discrepancy. The page now ranks on the exact values everywhere.

§ IV‑A
Population

What the bar selected was a tracker convention, not a quality level

Twelve of 38 clear 0.80, and every one is Hadoop‑ecosystem. The best project outside it — James — reaches 74.8%, against a median of 63.6% across all 38 and 44.8% across the 26 rejected.

Two facts keep that from being a story about engineering quality. First, ecosystem adjacency is not sufficient: Parquet fails at 29.1% and Accumulo at 43.0%, both squarely inside the Hadoop dependency graph. Second, the drop reasons are measured rather than inferred, because the probe counts GitHub‑issue references (#NNN, GH-NNN) per repository alongside Jira keys. Nine projects fail with github_issue_references_dominate — meaning the GitHub count exceeds the Jira count in that repository. ShardingSphere carries 30,746 GitHub‑issue references against 5 Jira keys in 49,111 commits.

Counting both channels is what turns each drop from an inference into a measurement, and it is what shows the bar is selecting which tracker a project uses. The industry‑donated projects are the sharpest case — ShardingSphere 0.01%, SkyWalking 0.3%, Pinot 5.2%, RocketMQ 5.7%, Dubbo 11.0% — they never adopted the Jira‑citation convention at all. Attrition is concentrated in the newest projects, which is the direction that matters for anyone building a corpus now.

The ecosystem label is a hand classification, and the paper says so

“Hadoop‑ecosystem” is not a field in any dataset — the author assigned it. The natural replacement is project generation: free metadata, no judgment, and a real mechanism, since projects predating the 2019–20 migration of ASF development to GitHub pull requests had longer under the Jira convention. Two generational variables were tested in its place (scripts/era_separation.py):

Neither measured variable separates the corpus as well as the hand label — and the graduation date is missing precisely where it would be needed: four of the twelve passing projects (Hive, HBase, ZooKeeper, Ozone) entered the ASF as Hadoop subprojects rather than incubator podlings and have no graduation date at all. The free metadata is not free for the group that matters.

The claim that survives without the label

12 of 38 pass, and the twelve are concentrated in a way no available metadata field predicts. That is the measured form, and it does not depend on the hand classification.

The paper then states its own exposure rather than defending it: reclassifying Drill — which requires neither HDFS nor YARN and is arguably outside the ecosystem — would falsify the “all twelve” form of the claim and put a non‑ecosystem project above the bar. Kylin and Sqoop are the same shape of exposure. Atlas is not, because at 78.1% it fails under either classification.

§ V
Taxonomy

Six ways a project silently fails to support an issue‑linked study

Every mode was discovered after selecting the project, by probing it. None is expressible in any published sampling frame. Three disqualify a project outright; three produce a plausible wrong number instead of an error — which is worse.

The distinction is what makes this a taxonomy rather than a list. Modes 1–3 mean the project is not a weak candidate; it is not a candidate. Scoring kata-containers as “0% traceable” would put it on the same axis as a project that tries and fails, and would put a number where a category belongs. Modes 4–6 are silent: they return a number that looks fine.

The one that should worry you most

A single‑key probe reads Hadoop at 26.2%. A four‑key probe of the same 28,290 commits reads 92.3%. A seven‑key probe reads 97.8%. These are not a rate and a correction to it — they are the same measurement under three key sets. Nothing in the first result signals the key set is wrong. A researcher who ran the single‑key probe would have dropped Hadoop as untraceable and never known why.

Mode 6 counted across the whole probe, not just its worked examples

The probed key sets were derived empirically from commit messages; the frozen public Jira corpus records every Apache project that existed at its snapshot. Intersecting the two turns mode 6 from an anecdote into a count: six of the keys this study probes have no project record in that corpus at all, and five of them are actually cited by commits.

Calcite is the clearest instance. OPTIQ was the project's name before it was renamed; its keys are cited 85 distinct times in the repository's history; no OPTIQ project exists in the tracker corpus. Enumerating the tracker's projects — the natural thing to do — cannot recover that key. It exists only in git, and a probe built the natural way would silently miss every commit citing it.

OZONE is retained deliberately. It appears in Ozone's probed key set and is cited by zero commits; every Jira reference in that repository is an HDDS key. The key set was fixed from a prefix scan before the counts were read, and removing it afterwards would be selection on the outcome.

And it operates inside the corpus that passed

Among the twelve eligible projects, Sqoop's commits cite 790 distinct keys of which 122 have no record in its tracker — mode 6 inside a project clearing the bar at 82.6%. Its ticket‑side rate is computed over the 668 that resolve, which means ticket‑side rates are, strictly, coverage of resolvable tickets.

One qualification the paper adds against itself

“The key set cannot be recovered from the tracker” stands — a key cited only in git is not in the tracker to be enumerated. But the unqualified claim that missing links are unrecoverable would overstate the gap. A substantial literature recovers missing issue–commit links without the commit message, beginning with ReLink (Wu et al., ESEC/FSE'11), by learning time proximity, author identity and textual similarity. Those methods recover links, not key sets, and they presuppose a project already known to be a candidate — which is the prior step this taxonomy is about. Modes 4–6 make a project's measured rate wrong; link recovery can raise the true rate afterwards.

§ IV‑C
Extension

The disagreement the paper reports instead of resolving

Measuring the ticket side across all 38 — including the 26 the bar rejected — rules out the assumption a selection bar encodes. It does not establish a null, and the two estimators point in opposite directions.

Reporting the divergence only on the twelve that passed leaves an obvious objection open: the 12.0–69.0% range is within‑passing variation, and says nothing about the 26 below the bar. So the ticket side was computed for all 38, taking denominators from the frozen public Jira corpus and aligning numerator and denominator in issue‑number space.

What this does and does not establish

At n = 33 the study can detect |ρ| ≥ 0.47 at 80% power. The interval admits everything from a moderate negative to a moderate positive association. A strong positive relationship — the assumption that a high commit‑side rate implies a usable ticket‑side rate — is ruled out. A null is not established.

Earlier drafts said the two rates are “uncorrelated” and that commit‑side “carries essentially no information.” Both overstate what n = 33 supports and are withdrawn.

The two intervals overlap and the pair is not formally contradictory, but the paper does not have one answer to give, and says so rather than manufacturing one. The decomposition that explains the difference: the correlation between commit‑side rate and commits‑per‑ticket is +0.455 within the eligible twelve and −0.717 across the 33, so the sign of the composite flips with the population rather than with the estimator. It is set out in full in paper/DIRECTION_TENSION.md.

Five projects have no usable rate and are excluded rather than scored zero, which is the taxonomy at work: Pinot (14 tracker issues), Dubbo (78) and RocketMQ (384) have denominators too small to carry a rate; ShardingSphere and SkyWalking have no project record for their probed key at all — modes 1 and 6, a category rather than a low number.

The selection effect on the dependent variable — § VII‑C

The obvious hope is that the visible minority is a random sample of the whole. In the one corpus where that could be tested, it is not. Architectural commits routed through a pull request are five times larger by churn and four times more abstraction‑heavy than those pushed directly to main.

The 31% that are visible are systematically the big, abstraction‑heavy ones; the 69% that are not are systematically the small, relocation‑shaped ones. That points the same way in every study that mines only what was recorded: architectural work will look larger, more deliberate and more discussed than it is. Bird et al. (ESEC/FSE'09) made this argument for bug‑fix datasets seventeen years earlier; the paper cites it as the prior statement of the principle rather than as a parallel.

Bounds that travel with the TypeScript column

One repository, one company: BearStudio/start-ui-web, 1,199 commits 2019‑12 → 2026‑07, 93% detector coverage, n = 28 architectural PRs. Marked EXPLORATORY throughout; every p‑value in it is uncorrected and hypothesis‑generating, so only counts and proportions enter the paper, never a test. The repository is also a starter template whose product is tracking the frontend stack, so migration‑driven churn is unusually large. And PR routing there is not arbitrary, which bounds what the 31% means — the 69%‑unreviewed figure is a statement about that repository's workflow, not a general rate.

Detector caveat: RefactoringMiner 3.1.4's TypeScript support was completed 2026‑05‑24, two months old at measurement, with no independent validation in the literature. One defect was traced and reported upstream — 160 interface → class false positives in a single commit, from type aliases having no representation in the tool's class model (tsantalis/RefactoringMiner#1124, filed 2026‑07‑25).

§ IV‑D
External check

The measure replicates across corpora seven years apart

The commit‑side rate is not a novel measure, and its agreement with an independently constructed dataset is the reason to trust the probe at all.

Flink's gap is scope, not error, and unlike in earlier drafts this is now tested rather than asserted. Re‑probing the earliest 12,419 commits — SEOSS's own change‑set count, and the only quantity the two studies share — closes it to four ten‑thousandths of a percentage point. The remaining 25,800 commits run at 77.62%, and Flink's by‑year series shows why: 0.0% in each of 2010–2013, 19.4% in 2014, 66.0% in 2015, and 70–88% every year since. Five of five overlapping projects agree once scope is matched.

On the exactness of that match, which invites suspicion and should not

The quantity is a deterministic count — commits whose message matches a key pattern, over a fixed and identically bounded commit range — not an estimate, and it carries no sampling error. Two correct implementations of the same well‑specified count should agree exactly; a disagreement would indicate a difference in specification, not noise. Exact agreement is only suspicious between estimators that have sampling error, and this has none. The two figures also reach us by independent paths: SEOSS's is transcribed from their published table, and 41.9841% is a fresh scan of a clone at a pinned sha. What the match confirms is scope alignment, and nothing beyond it — in particular it does not validate the ticket‑side estimator.

The ticket‑side estimator, and the honest version of its validation

Two approximations are stacked in the 38‑project extension: number‑capping (using cited keys ≤ N over N, rather than intersecting with the real key set) and snapshot substitution (the frozen tracker's N rather than a live one). Earlier drafts quoted a validation figure that covers only the first.

Quote 1.76 pp / 11.91 pp for anything in Table III. The favourable reading is also true and is the one to lead with — eleven of twelve land within 2.93 pp, and the twelfth is Kylin, whose branch truncation is the same defect showing up a second time.

And the estimator is applied entirely outside the range it was validated on

The twelve validation projects span commit‑side 82.6–98.3%. The twenty‑one that carry the association span 10.5–78.1%. The ranges are disjoint: 0 of 21 applied projects fall inside the validated one.

There is a plausible argument that this does not matter — the error mechanism is tracker‑numbering density rather than commit hygiene, and within the twelve the error does not track the commit‑side rate (ρ = −0.16). That is an argument, not a validation, and the paper makes it as such. Any claim whose margin is smaller than 11.91 pp is not safe on this estimator.

The matcher itself was measured, not assumed

Precision 195 of 200 = 97.5% (Wilson 95% CI 94.3–98.9%), corpus‑weighted 97.7%, on a seeded 200‑commit manual sample across the twelve eligible projects. The residual is four reverts and one backport.

Two of the five enumerated failure categories could not have fired. version_string is near‑unreachable given a pattern demanding an uppercase key followed by - and digits; foreign_key is unreachable for 11 of the 12, because each project is probed with its own key set and only Ozone is multi‑key with both keys its own. The single‑key → multi‑key result on Hadoop is therefore not validated by that sample, Hadoop being the only true monorepo in the study and not among the twelve.

One construct limit is recorded rather than corrected. A key matched from a branch name counts as a citation — merge subjects (Merge branch 'master' into KNOX-998-Package_Restructuring) and svn trailers (.../branches/TEZ-1@1471779) both occur in the sample. The ticket association is correct; the commit need not implement the ticket. The five failure categories were fixed before labelling and do not cover this case, so it was counted genuine and disclosed rather than assigned an invented category.

§ VI
Threats

The error ledger

Several items below are things this study got wrong and corrected. They are stated as the paper's credibility, not as its embarrassments — a methods paper about record quality that concealed its own record would be self‑refuting.

Kylin's 12.0% ticket‑side was the paper's lead illustration while the frozen 0.04% was dismissed as an artefact. Both are the same artefact — a pinned branch covering 7.5% of the repository. Wrong validation figure
0.45 pp / 2.14 pp was quoted for a table using a different, stacked approximation. The end‑to‑end error is 1.76 pp / 11.91 pp.§ VI‑A
Six operationalisations closed
The study began as an attempt to measure signal‑to‑action latency. Six operationalisations were tested and closed, each with a named cause: a Cox model on review discussion (discussion‑volume confound); module centrality as a predictor of hesitation (null once tier is controlled); maintainer concentration (collinear with module size, ρ = −0.86); an abstraction‑vs‑relocation split (×1.54 [0.97, 2.43], defined post hoc); a decade‑long process‑improvement trend that holds to 2019 and dissolves after 2020; and a portable module‑tier rule that replicated 0 of 3 in a held‑out corpus. What survived is the observation that made all six fragile: the sampling frame itself is the finding.§ I · figures/eligibility_funnel.png · replication/REPLICATION.md
A threshold held when lowering it would have helped
A separate frozen threshold — vendor share ≥ 0.40 for the module‑tier rule — was held through a held‑out replication in which lowering it would have rescued two of five projects (HBase, maximum share 0.33; Phoenix, which flagged nothing). It was not lowered, and the rule then failed 0 of 3 where it could be tested. Recorded deliberately, because a pre‑registered threshold never tested against temptation is not evidence of anything.§ VI‑F · replication/REPLICATION.md
A 93%‑missing year, found and repaired
The first RefactoringMiner run lost three chunks to hangs, and the loss was clustered: 75 of 81 commits in 2024 (93%) were absent, against 2–8% in every other year. Every temporal result from that pass was invalid, including a “zero architectural commits in 2024” that was a measurement hole. A targeted re‑run recovered 68 commits and lifted coverage 88% → 93%. Coverage loss in chunked detector runs must be checked for clustering, not merely reported as a percentage.§ VI‑D
A bias test that was wrong, kept as a caution
The natural worry — that detector loss is biased toward large commits — was tested and rejected: lost commits are smaller (median churn 14 lines against 32, p = 0.0022) with no enrichment in the top churn decile. But a first attempt compared against git log --all, contaminating the “missing” set with 930 commits on other branches that were never in scope, and produced the opposite, spurious conclusion. The comparison set for a coverage‑bias test must be exactly the range the tool was asked to analyse.§ VI‑D
A coincidence that looks like tuning
In the LLM‑rater pilot, six comments change label under the opposite ordering of two codebook rules, and five fall in the flagged bucket — moving the κ ceiling from 0.491 to 0.840. Five is also exactly the count that maximises the ceiling over every possible count (four gives 0.765, six 0.837, seven 0.833). The six were selected by a stated semantic criterion and are individually defensible, the finding is robust at 0.833–0.840 across ±2 — but the criterion was applied by the same party that reported the resulting number, and it landed on the global maximum. Stated here rather than left for a reader to find.§ VI‑E · audit/JUDGMENTS.md § 3

Aliasing measured, not assumed
The TypeScript pilot found one developer appearing as two email addresses with opposite workflow habits, which corrupted every author‑level statistic in that pass. Hadoop's raw aliasing is worse — 1,035 emails resolve to 797 identities, and 96 emails span multiple author names covering 35.2% of the corpus — but the effect on derived measures is null (mean top1_share 0.1545 by name vs 0.1495 by email, paired Wilcoxon p = 0.3651). Single‑rater limits
Inter‑rater agreement cannot be computed by one person, so no measure requiring manual coding carries a claim in this paper. The codebook's keyword rule for structural review discussion is ≈25% precise (5 of 20 flagged genuinely structural) with a ≈5% miss rate, on a 40‑comment single‑rater pass with no κ. Held‑out discipline is mechanical

Twelve projects is not enough, and more would not fix it
Between‑project correlation caps the effective sample at P/ICC whatever the corpus size: at P = 12 and ICC = 0.02 the ceiling is 600 against the 769 independent tickets the effect needed at 80% power. The direction of the bias is bad — these twelve share contributors, committers, review norms and in several cases build and CI infrastructure, so this sample's ICC exceeds a random sample's. § VI‑A
For reviewers

What is safe to cite from this paper

The paper states its own margin rule explicitly, which is unusual and worth honouring: any claim whose margin is smaller than 11.91 pp is not safe on the frozen estimator.

Safe

  • The ceiling identity. TRR ≤ CSR × commits/tickets. Exact arithmetic, not estimated, not measured — it holds by construction for any project anywhere.
  • The eligibility result. 12 of 38 clear 0.80. Commit‑side only, deterministic count, cross‑validated against SEOSS 33 at matched scope on five of five overlapping projects.
  • The six‑mode taxonomy and the claim that none of it is expressible in any published sampling frame.
  • The live divergence on the twelve. CSR 82.6–98.3% against TRRlive 12.0–69.0%, both measured directly with no estimator in between.
  • The fill decomposition — ceiling varies 6.0×, fill varies 1.19× across the eligible twelve.
  • The counts and proportions from the TypeScript corpus: 3 of 96, 7%, 30 of 96. Never its p‑values.

Not safe

  • Any per‑project ticket‑side comparison in Table III closer than ~12 pp. The end‑to‑end estimator error is 11.91 pp at worst.
  • The passing‑versus‑dropped median gap of 0.6 pp. Far inside the noise, and the paper reports it as such.
  • Any claim of a null association between the two rates. n = 33 detects only |ρ| ≥ 0.47 at 80% power, and the live measurement on twelve points the other way at +0.518.
  • Anything resting on manual coding. Single rater, no κ. The codebook figures are findings about the instrument, not instruments.
  • The single‑key → multi‑key Hadoop result as matcher‑validated. The 200‑commit sample could not have tested it — Hadoop is the only true monorepo and is not among the twelve.
  • Any temporal claim after 2021 in the Apache arm. The CI channel terminates at 0 of 43, and the phase decomposition rests on six tickets.

One determination is explicitly left open

Whether the ticket realisation rate is the same quantity Bachmann et al. (FSE'10) measured — they engaged a core Apache HTTP Server developer to annotate 493 commits exhaustively and found only 47.6% of bug‑fix‑related commits are documented in the bug tracker — or a distinct one, is not settled in this draft. The argument for distinctness is that this study's denominator admits every issue type and every status and conditions on nothing, whereas Bachmann conditions on bugs and bug‑fix commits. That argument has not been adjudicated against the paper's own text, and the naming claim stands or falls with it. Marked [DETERMINATION PENDING] in the manuscript rather than resolved in the author's favour.

§ VII
Discussion

If you are about to build an issue‑anchored corpus

This is the part that costs a reader nothing and saves them a month.

Measure the side of the rate your design actually samples on

A commit‑side rate answers “if I start from a commit, can I find its ticket?” A design that starts from tickets needs the ticket realisation rate, and the first does not imply the second. But the more useful advice is the ceiling: report commits per ticket beside whichever rate you publish. It costs nothing, it bounds the ticket‑side rate before any measurement, and no published linkage rate carries it.

Report the bar, the attrition, and the rejected candidates

The dropped projects are the informative part. That 26 of 38 Apache candidates are unusable, and that the twelve survivors are one ecosystem, is a fact about the population that a paper reporting only its final corpus cannot convey — and it is a fact each such paper independently rediscovers and does not write down.

Four fields would close most of the gap in the standard sampling frame

GHS is the standard sampling tool for MSR studies; it indexed 735,669 repositories as published in 2021 and exposes 35 fields per record today. Only totalIssues and openIssues touch issues at all, and both are GitHub‑issue counts. There is no field for issue‑tracker type, external tracker usage, issue–commit linkage, traceability, or commit‑message convention. The one adjacent feature, filtering by issue label, is described in the paper itself as still under development.

#fieldwhat it catchescost
1Dominant reference channel — Jira‑key citations against GitHub‑issue references, counted, not assumedMode 1 · the bar selecting a tracker rather than a disciplinegit log
2The tracker's project‑key setMode 5 · concurrent siblings sharing one repositoryone paginated tracker call
3The key set actually appearing in commit messagesMode 6 · superseded records. The only thing that catches them — those keys exist in git and nowhere elsegit log
4The rate recomputed over recent historyMode 4 · a convention that changed mid‑history and is averaged away by a single rategit log

All four come from a --filter=tree:0 bare clone plus one tracker listing. On this corpus those 38 clones came to 228 MB, a mean of 6.0 MB per project, against the 4.3 GB the original working‑tree sweep took — a 19× reduction. Fields 1, 3 and 4 need only commit messages: no trees, no blobs, no working tree. The claim is not that this is free at the scale of 735,669 repositories; it is that the marginal cost is the same order as what GHS already does per repository, and that nobody has to guess.

Three ways out of the corpus limit, with their costs

ALower the bar and model the measurement errorthe one to avoid drifting into

At 60% another nine projects qualify and the corpus widens beyond one ecosystem. The cost is a biased sample of tickets within each project — which § VII‑C shows is real rather than hypothetical.

BChange the outcome so it needs no ticketsstrongest study · largest rebuild

Anything computed purely from git dissolves both the traceability filter and the ecosystem clustering at once. The cost is the creation‑to‑first‑commit clock, and with it the ability to measure waiting at all.

CAccept ecosystem‑bounded scope and say sohonest · immediately actionable

Register as a study of one ecosystem and twelve projects, and state the boundary as a finding rather than discovering it in review.

It does not claim that Jira‑citing projects are better engineered, or that projects on GitHub Issues are less traceable in any sense that matters to their own maintainers. The convention this research needs is not a quality signal; it is a research affordance, and its decline is a cost to researchers rather than to the projects. And it does not offer a fix. The six modes are diagnosable, not repairable: a project whose tracker is downstream of its repository will not start citing keys because a researcher would like it to.
