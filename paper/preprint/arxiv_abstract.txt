Empirical studies of architectural change read a project's own records and assume
the record traces the work. We measure that assumption across three record
channels and two ecosystems.

We probed 38 Apache projects against a traceability bar of 0.80, pre-registered
before any project was cloned and never moved. Twelve passed, and no project
outside the Hadoop ecosystem cleared the bar. The channel the literature
publishes is not the channel an issue-anchored study needs: the commit-side rate
(what fraction of commits cite a ticket) runs 82.6-98.3% across the eligible
twelve, while the ticket-side rate (what fraction of tickets ever receive a
citing commit) runs 12.0-69.0%. Apache Hive cites a ticket in 97.0% of its 18,213
commits and realises 55.8% of its 29,635 tickets.

Most of that gap is arithmetic rather than a difference in discipline. A citing
commit adds at most one new distinct ticket, so the ticket-side rate cannot
exceed commit-side x commits / tickets. That ceiling binds for all twelve
eligible projects, ranging 13.7-81.6%, and every project reaches 80-95% of it: a
5.8x spread in the ticket-side rate is a 6.0x spread in the ceiling and a 1.19x
spread in what remains. Extending the measurement to all 38 projects, including
the 26 the bar rejected, we find no detectable association between the two rates
(Spearman rho = -0.010, n = 33, 95% CI [-0.35, +0.34]); the study can detect
|rho| >= 0.47 at 80% power, so a strong positive association is ruled out but a
null is not established.

We give a taxonomy of six mechanisms by which a project silently fails to support
an issue-linked study. Three are disqualifying; three produce a plausible wrong
number rather than an error, and none is expressible in any published sampling
frame. Project eligibility for issue-linked research cannot be established from
metadata.
